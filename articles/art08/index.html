USA Today Co. & Palantir

There comes a moment, when observing the transformations taking place in the world of news, when one gets the impression that something is changing not only on the surface of the news, but in the very structure of the relationship between those who produce it and those who read it as if a slow and almost imperceptible shift were redrawing the rules of a pact that, for decades, we have taken for granted. The decision by USA Today Co. (the country’s largest newspaper chain) to form a partnership with Palantir (a company that for years has operated in contexts where data management is intertwined with government, military, and surveillance operations) seems to fit squarely within this kind of change, because it involves not just a business strategy or a technological upgrade, but touches on a deeper dimension: the one that defines the trust between a newspaper and its audience.

The news, announced during a call with investors and communicated to employees via an email sent just twenty minutes earlier, describes an agreement that should allow USA Today Co. to analyze reader behavior in a more sophisticated way, transforming every visit, every click, and every second spent on a page into a signal to be linked to others, thereby creating a sort of detailed map of the audience’s habits. The stated goal is to monetize that behavior more quickly and accurately by automating processes that, until recently, were handled manually, and by seeking to offset the decline in traffic from search engines, a phenomenon affecting the entire industry and forcing newsrooms to chase an increasingly elusive readership.

Yet, the moment one tries to look beyond the surface, a tension emerges that cannot be ignored, because Palantir is not just any company, but one that has built its reputation by collaborating with government agencies, defense departments, and security organizations, developing tools capable of cross-referencing enormous amounts of data to identify patterns, suspects, and targets. It is a company that, over the years, has been associated with surveillance operations, deportations, military campaigns and that carries with it an image that is difficult to reconcile with the idea of a local newspaper chronicling the life of a community. When a media giant decides to entrust its knowledge of its readers to a company that operates in such contexts, something inevitably shifts, not only from an ethical standpoint but also from a symbolic one, because the reader is no longer merely a recipient of content but becomes a set of signals to be interpreted, a profile to be reconstructed, and a potential customer to be optimized.

It is no surprise, then, that more than 800 employees have decided to sign a public statement calling on the company to end the partnership, emphasizing that this decision risks undermining readers’ trust and exposing data security to new risks especially at a time when protecting sensitive information is one of the most delicate challenges facing journalism. Their concern does not stem from a blanket rejection of technology, nor from nostalgia for an analog past that will never return; rather, it arises from the awareness that journalism rests on a fragile balance built on transparency, independence, accountability and that any factor that disrupts this balance risks having consequences that extend beyond the technical realm to the cultural and democratic spheres.

What is striking about this whole affair is the way the news was communicated to employees: a single sentence buried among financial updates, an announcement that almost seems designed to downplay the significance of the decision, as if it were a minor detail rather than a change that could redefine the way the newspaper understands and interprets its audience. It’s a move that reveals a growing disconnect between those who make the decisions and those who experience the consequences of those choices every day, probably a disconnect that has widened in recent years due to budget cuts, restructuring, and bets on artificial intelligence that often seem driven more by the need to survive than by a clear vision for the future of journalism.

Meanwhile, official statements continue to reiterate that the data will remain the company’s property, that privacy will be respected, and that the partnership with Palantir is merely a way to be more competitive in a challenging market. Taken individually, these words might even be reassuring, but they lose their impact when spoken in a context where newsrooms have seen their resources dwindle, pressure mount, and the rules of the game constantly change. It’s hard to believe that everything will remain under control when, in recent years, so many things have changed without those working in newsrooms having any real say in the matter.

And then there is another element, more subtle but equally significant: the fact that Palantir is, at times, the subject of the very news stories that USA Today Co. publishes. Collaborating with an entity that is both the subject of its own narrative and the focus of an investigation creates a tension that cannot be ignored, like a sort of ethical short circuit that risks calling editorial independence into question, even when it is strongly reaffirmed in official statements. It is as if the newspaper were walking on ground that, while appearing solid, hides cracks that could widen the moment the public senses that the distance between the observer and the observed is narrowing too much.

In the end, what remains is a complex feeling, made up of questions that have no immediate answers. One wonders what it really means to turn every digital action into a signal to be analyzed, what it entails to entrust readers’ data to a company that operates in surveillance contexts, and what happens when a newspaper decides to treat its audience not only as a community to be informed, but as a set of data to be optimized. One wonders whether this is an inevitable direction or whether, somewhere, there is still the possibility of imagining a different relationship between writers and readers, maybe one less mediated by algorithms and more grounded in mutual trust.

This is not a story that ends with a moral or a clear conclusion. It is a narrative that continues to unfold, intertwined with the future of journalism, the transformation of platforms, the fragility of local newsrooms, and the fear that technology might become too powerful an intermediary between people and the information they seek. It is a news story that, the more you look at it, the more it seems to speak to something that concerns us all. The way we tell our stories, the way we are observed, and the way, without realizing it, we are changing our relationship with what we call “the news.”
