LG TVs

At a time when home technology seems to have reached such a level of integration that it blends into our daily routines, becoming almost a natural extension of our lives, the controversy surrounding LG televisions has taken on significance that goes far beyond a mere technical issue, because it has brought back to the forefront of the discussion a topic that, despite having been around for years, rarely manages to emerge with the force needed to compel us to look at it without distractions. The relationship between what we believe we control and what, in reality, is watching us while we use it or even when we think we aren’t using it at all. Investigations conducted by Gamers Nexus, in collaboration with Level1Techs and several independent researchers, have in fact shown that certain models of LG OLED TVs do not merely collect information about what is displayed on the screen, but appear to be an integral part of a sort of continuous monitoring of the home’s digital environment as if the television had become an active node in a broader network, capable of observing, categorize, and interpret what is happening around it even when the screen is off, the device is in standby mode, or, in some cases, even disconnected from the network.

According to tests conducted using tools such as Wireshark, televisions scan the local network for devices, identifying phones, smartwatches, printers, thermostats, IoT devices, and even hardware belonging to people who were not involved in the investigation as if the TV, in an effort to better understand the context in which it operates, were ultimately reconstructing a digital map of the home environment. This map does not merely list the devices present but also includes internal IP addresses, signal strength of surrounding networks, channels in use, and information that may even suggest the physical location of the home.

Added to this is ACR technology, which creates audio and video fingerprints of whatever is being displayed (regardless of the source) and sends them to LG Ad Solutions’ advertising systems, an infrastructure that reaches hundreds of millions of televisions worldwide, fueling a business model built on the ability to transform every viewing moment into data useful for advertising profiling. This is where corporate slogans like “we own the glass” or “we know who’s in the LG household” come into play, phrases that, when viewed in the context of a business strategy, may seem like simple marketing metaphors, but which take on a much more concrete meaning, almost suggesting that the TV does more than just display content, but it becomes a vantage point through which the company can analyze and monetize what happens in the user’s home.

The most sensitive aspect of the entire issue, however, concerns the microphone. The researchers observed that the TV continues to capture audio even when the screen is off, that the microphone remains active for several seconds after a voice command ends, and that the recordings are stored locally and then automatically uploaded when the Internet connection becomes available again, as if the device, even though it is not designed to record ambient conversations, still ends up capturing snippets of speech that could include people present in the room but not directly involved in the interaction. LG maintains that its TVs do not record ambient conversations, and technically the distinction between voice commands and conversations is relevant, however the fact that the microphone remains active longer than necessary, that recordings are transcribed into text and saved in the device’s logs, and that all of this occurs even in standby mode, has fueled a debate that goes beyond technology alone, but the relationship of trust between users and manufacturers especially when one considers that LG televisions are found not only in homes but also in hotels, clinics, and meeting rooms, places where the presence of an active microphone, even for just a few seconds, can take on a completely different meaning.

The online reactions were immediate, with some users stating they no longer want to buy LG products, others describing the TVs’ behavior as “extremely disturbing,” and still others wondering how it’s possible that a device purchased with the intention of owning it could instead behave as if the manufacturer were the one owning the user or their habits. In the meantime, it is recommended to disconnect TVs from the internet, use external devices for streaming, regularly check privacy settings, and consider that, in an industry where profit margins are increasingly slim, data collection may have become the real currency of exchange, cleverly hidden behind updates to the terms of use.

As the debate continues and other companies in the industry are called into question amid speculation that similar practices may be widespread, the question remains as to where the line should be drawn between smart features and privacy. After all, it’s a question that, as is often the case when it comes to home technology, doesn’t end with a definitive verdict but remains unresolved, ready to resurface every time a new screen turns on, or perhaps every time a new screen turns off, suggesting that the issue will continue to evolve alongside the devices that fill our homes.
